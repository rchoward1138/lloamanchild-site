WHY HE DID THIS

A bonus chapter from Life Lessons of a Man-Child

Why He Did This

By R.C. Howard

He told himself he was writing a book.

That sounded simple enough. Respectable, even. The kind of thing people say when they want to feel productive, thoughtful, maybe even a little literary. But the truth was messier than that. He wasn't just writing a book. He was digging through old rooms in his own mind, opening doors he had left shut for years, brushing dust off moments he had carried around so long they no longer felt like memories at all. They felt like furniture. Permanent. Heavy. Easy to stop noticing until you tried to move them.

Some of those memories still made him laugh. Some still stung. A few did both.

What he found, over and over, was that the stories he thought were small were never really small at all. A childhood moment. A movie line. A family habit. A joke told a hundred times. A fear he never named properly when he was younger. A kindness he didn't fully appreciate until much later. These things had been following him all along, like background characters waiting for their turn to speak.

So why did he do it?

Partly because time has a rude way of speeding up. One day you're a kid with a head full of movies, monsters, make-believe, and impossible futures. Then one day you look up and realize you're the age your parents seemed when they already knew everything, except now you know they didn't, and neither do you. You just get better at pretending in public.

Partly because he had stories in him that had been sitting on shelves too long. Not polished stories. Not stories born in some perfect writer's cabin with rain tapping on the windows and a typewriter humming like a dream. Real stories. Crooked stories. Funny stories. Wounded stories. Stories that smelled more like old paperback books, Atari plastic, summer sweat, church pews, cigarette smoke from somebody else's decade, and the faint static of late-night television.

Partly because he wanted to make sense of himself.

Not in the neat, clinical way people talk about healing, like life can be labeled, filed, and tucked into a color-coded drawer. He wanted to make sense of the contradictions. How a man can grow older without ever fully growing up. How grief and humor can sit at the same table. How faith can dim, flicker, and return. How the things that shape us most are often the things that seemed ordinary at the time. How someone can still be a child at heart without being childish in spirit.

And partly, if he was being honest, because he was afraid not to.

If he never wrote these stories down, they would still live inside him, but in a different way. Less like companions. More like unfinished business.

This book became a way of remembering. A way of honoring. A way of sorting through what was real, what was felt, what was lost, what was funny, and what still mattered. It gave shape to people he loved, moments he carried, and truths he had spent years circling without always realizing it.

It also forced him to confront the fact that memory is not a filing cabinet. It's a jukebox with a busted light. Sometimes it plays the right song. Sometimes it skips. Sometimes it gives you something you didn't know you needed. But when it works, really works, it can take you somewhere no map can.

He wrote this book because stories had always been part of how he survived, how he made sense of the world, how he connected what he felt to what he lived. He wrote it because imagination had never been a phase for him. It had been a lifeline. He wrote it because growing older had taught him something childhood never could: that some of the things people dismiss as sentimental, silly, nostalgic, or soft are often the very things that keep a person whole.

Most of all, he wrote it because he finally understood that the stories collecting dust in his life were not waiting to become someone else's masterpiece. They were waiting to become his.

And maybe that was the lesson hiding underneath all of it.

He didn't write the book because he had all the answers.

He wrote it because the questions still mattered.
