Games & growing up

Can’t Get Past the Waterfall

By R. C. Howard · About 7 minutes

All stories ↗

There are certain sounds that never leave you.

A screen flickering to life in a dim room.
The hard click of a controller in your hands.
The staccato burst of an 8-bit machine gun.
That unmistakable Contra music charging out of an old TV speaker like it had somewhere to be.

And somewhere in the middle of all that noise, the voice of my dad.

Not giving a lecture.
Not laying down the law.
Not speaking from some distant mountaintop of fatherly wisdom.

Just talking to me like we were teammates.

That may not sound like much to anyone who didn't grow up when I did, but to me, it was everything.

Contra was never just a game in our house. It was a ritual. A challenge. A language. A test of memory, timing, patience, and endurance. Mostly endurance. Because Contra, like life and many household appliances built in the 1980s, did not care about your feelings. It was there to punish weakness, expose bad reflexes, and make sure you respected the waterfall.

That waterfall.

If you know, you know.

To this day, "Can't get past the waterfall" means more to me than a difficult level in an old Nintendo game. It means being stuck but not quitting. It means dying for stupid reasons, then trying again. It means frustration turning into laughter. It means the kind of repetition that should have driven us insane somehow becoming memory instead.

Mostly, it means my dad.

When I was young, he wasn't just my father. He was my best friend, even before I fully understood how rare that was. There are dads who sit in the background of childhood like stern furniture. Solid, present, useful, but distant. Mine wasn't like that. Mine played. Mine laughed. Mine got invested. Mine grabbed a second controller and entered the arena with me.

And Contra was one of the places where that bond lived.

I don't remember every conversation we had while playing. Memory doesn't work that way, no matter how badly a writer wishes it would. But I remember the feeling of it. I remember the rhythm. One of us moving too fast. The other hanging back. A groan when somebody wasted a good weapon. A yell when things got chaotic. The mutual blame, the mutual forgiveness, the immediate restart.

That was our version of quality time.

No speeches. No dramatic heart-to-hearts. No violins swelling in the background while father and son suddenly understand one another under a sunset. We had aliens, bullets, spread guns, panic, and a side-scrolling battlefield that somehow made room for love.

Maybe that's part of why the game mattered so much.

Boys and men are not always taught to connect through words. Sometimes they connect through tasks. Through motion. Through shared focus. Through fixing something, building something, watching something, driving somewhere, or in our case, trying not to die every eight seconds in front of a blinking television set.

In that world, my dad and I were equals in the best way a kid can experience equality. He wasn't above me. He was beside me. He wasn't watching my childhood from a distance. He was inside it.

And like all the best parts of childhood, I assumed it would last forever.

Then life did what life does. It widened.

I moved out at twenty-three, full of all the old, familiar ideas about what becoming a man was supposed to mean. You leave home. You make your way. You see the world. You build a life. You chase love, work, independence, identity. You gather friends. Or at least people you think are friends until time reveals they were really just temporary extras wandering through your movie.

That's another story.

The point is, distance entered the picture.

Not the dramatic kind. Not some broken, bitter separation. Just the ordinary distance adulthood introduces when schedules fill up, responsibilities multiply, and the days begin slipping past faster than they used to. You mean to call more. You mean to visit more. You mean to say the things that sit just below the surface. Then another week passes. Then another month. Then suddenly you're measuring life in holidays, obligations, and the occasional "we really need to do this more often."

The strange thing is, none of that changed what I knew.

My dad was still my best friend.

That truth didn't vanish just because I was busy trying to become an adult. It didn't disappear because I found love, built routines, or spent time learning the hard way that not every friendship deserves the name. It just became quieter. Less obvious in the day-to-day. Buried under grown-up noise.

But the older I get, the more I realize that some of the deepest relationships don't stop being real when they stop being constant. They just wait. They live under the surface, steady as bedrock, ready to come rushing back through one memory, one joke, one shared phrase.

One game.

Contra became one of those touchstones for me. Not because it was the best game ever made, though it certainly earned its place in the 8-bit hall of fame. And not just because of the famous code, that sacred little cheat sequence passed between kids like scripture: up, up, down, down, left, right, left, right, B, A, Start. The Konami Code was magic, sure. It gave us thirty lives, which felt less like a cheat and more like mercy.

But what stayed with me was what that code represented.

A map.
A ritual.
A secret handshake.
A reminder that sometimes survival depends on knowing the pattern.

Life has patterns too, though it rarely hands them to you in such a neat arrangement.

There are ups and downs, obviously. More than we'd like. Lefts and rights when you're trying to figure out which direction actually matters. Bs and As, whatever those become in a grown man's life. Bills and anxieties, maybe. Burdens and acceptance. Bruises and affection. Then, if you're lucky, a moment when everything finally aligns and something in you says, "Let's start."

Maybe that's why the code still hits me the way it does.

Not because it gave me extra lives in a video game, but because it reminds me that none of us make it through on one life alone. We survive on retries. On grace. On help. On memory. On the people who stood beside us when we were young and made hard things feel beatable.

My dad did that for me.

He did it with more than games, of course. But games were one of the clearest places I could see it. They stripped life down to basics. Stay alert. Learn the pattern. Don't panic. Keep moving. Laugh when things go wrong. Start over when you have to. And if somebody you love is beside you, the whole level changes.

That's what Contra was.

Not just a cartridge. Not just a nostalgic relic from the Nintendo era. Not just another title from the old days when pixels were blocky and imaginations did the heavy lifting.

It was a battlefield where a father and son learned, without ever needing to say it outright, that they were good together.

That they liked each other.

That they belonged on the same side.

I mentioned Contra in my memoir because leaving it out would have felt dishonest. It wasn't just one of the games I played. It was one of the places I lived. One of the places my dad and I met, over and over again, somewhere between alien gunfire and the eternal humiliation of not getting past the waterfall.

And maybe that's what nostalgia really is when it's at its best.

Not an escape from the present.
Not some desperate wish to go backward.
But a way of recognizing the moments that built you before you knew you were being built.

I left home at twenty-three to see the world and become a man.

Fair enough. That's what young men do. Or at least what they tell themselves while loading up their car and trying to act more certain than they feel.

But no matter where I went, no matter what I found, lost, misunderstood, or outgrew, some part of me was always still sitting there with my dad in front of that Nintendo, gripping a controller, waiting for the next round, believing that with enough lives and the right person beside you, anything could be beaten.

Even the waterfall.
